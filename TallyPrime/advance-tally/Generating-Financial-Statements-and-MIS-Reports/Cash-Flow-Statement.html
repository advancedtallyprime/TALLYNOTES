1. Core Concept

A Cash Flow Statement tracks the actual inflows and outflows of Cash and Cash Equivalents during a financial period. While the Profit & Loss account operates on accrual accounting, the Cash Flow Statement reveals whether the business generated real cash from operations to meet its ongoing liabilities.

2. Three Key Activity Classifications (AS-3 / Ind-AS 7)

Activity Type Inflow Examples Outflow Examples
1. Operating Activities Cash sales, collections from trade debtors, commission received. Payments to trade suppliers, staff salaries, factory rent, income tax paid.
2. Investing Activities Sale of fixed assets (machinery, land), return on investment (dividends/interest). Purchase of plant, machinery, computers, long-term capital investments.
3. Financing Activities Issue of equity shares, fresh bank term loans, partner capital additions. Repayment of bank loan principal, dividend distribution, owner drawings.

3. Generating Cash Flow in TallyPrime

Navigation: Gateway of Tally → Display More Reports → Cash Flow.
Shortcut: Alt + G → Type Cash Flow.

Monthly Cash Flow Breakdown:

  • TallyPrime generates an intuitive month-by-month bar chart and numerical breakdown of Net Inflow vs. Net Outflow.
  • Press Enter on any month to inspect individual cash and bank transactions contributing to the flow.
  • Use Funds Flow (Alt + G → "Funds Flow") to analyze long-term working capital shifts.

4. Interview Questions & Answers

Q1. Can a profitable company go bankrupt? How does Cash Flow Statement reveal this?

Ans: Yes. A company may show high Net Profit on accrual basis due to credit sales, but if debtors fail to pay and cash is trapped in unsold stock, the company will face a cash crunch. The Cash Flow Statement highlights negative Operating Cash Flow despite positive accounting profits.

Q2. How is Cash Flow different from Funds Flow in TallyPrime?

Ans: Cash Flow tracks changes strictly in Cash and Bank balances (narrow liquidity), while Funds Flow tracks changes in total Working Capital (Current Assets minus Current Liabilities).
